Research Interests

Language models are trained, adapted and deployed on tight budgets of data and compute, and my research asks where those budgets are best spent. During training, which few examples can stand in for a large batch? During adaptation and inference, when does an extra signal, such as a teacher or a verifier, change the outcome? I turn these questions into algorithms using submodular optimization, non-convex optimization and optimal transport, aiming for methods that are cheap to run and come with guarantees.

Data-efficient training and adaptation. The first question is order. OnlineSubmod [1] treats curriculum design as a bandit over submodular objectives, steered by validation feedback. Within a batch, PartitionSel [2] trains on a fraction of the examples while keeping the full batch's gradient, and per-domain budgets limit interference between domains. One level up, TaskPGM [3] and graph-structured allocation [4] derive fine-tuning mixtures from affinities between tasks. When tasks arrive one after another, the scarce resource is the model's own capacity: Sculpting Subspaces [5] restricts new updates to directions that leave prior knowledge intact. I am now studying how spectral optimizers such as Muon change what a subset has to preserve.

Fair coverage. A subset can match the data on average and still lose its smallest groups. UniPROT [6] guards against this by selecting equally weighted prototypes through partial optimal transport, with a (1−1/e) guarantee. The same failure appears as data-poor clients in federated learning, which CoreSet-PFedBayes [7] addresses, and as rare labels in multi-label classification, which FairPO [8] addresses.

Robust generalization and verifiable reasoning. Choosing an example settles what to learn from, but not how. In distillation [9], the student's early layer representations decide, example by example, how far to follow the teacher over the label. Under distribution drift [10], the corresponding choice is how much weight older data deserves. At inference, the extra signal is a verifier. InterWhen [11] checks reasoning while it is being produced and corrects mistakes before later steps inherit them. A question I keep returning to is which steps are worth checking when verification itself is costly.

More details and future directions are in my research statement.
